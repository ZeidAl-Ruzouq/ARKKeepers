The Arkkeepers: research dossier

Project and method

Journey of the Ark of the Covenant · Who Wrote the Bible? · Johns Hopkins University · Professor Alice Mandell.

Team: Zeid Al Ruzouq, Jackson Hanselman, and Thomas Lapp. Research checked 1 November 2026; gameplay revision checked 2 November 2026. This is a source-linked development dossier, intended to support further university research and human revision; it is not a claim to have consulted every work in full.

The game follows the order of the final biblical narrative from Exodus to 1 Kings. That order is a literary itinerary, not an independently established chronology of events or of the composition of the texts. It brings together passages with different emphases and possible compositional histories. The game does not assign absolute dates to the wilderness, crossing, capture, or Temple installation. It does not propose that the same physical object has been traced archaeologically through every episode.

Three evidence categories govern both the interface and this dossier. Biblical description means a claim made by a cited text. Scholarly interpretation means a named scholar’s argument, which may be disputed. Interpretive reconstruction means geometry, landscape, sound, timing, or other teaching choices made for this game. These categories are not degrees of religious value: they distinguish what a source can establish for historical inquiry.

The World English Bible at eBible.org supplies linked primary passages and is openly available. Interface wording is principally original paraphrase. The USCCB’s New American Bible Revised Edition was also checked for selected chapters and translation notes. Verse numbering, Hebrew terms, and translations can differ. Each passage link allows readers to assess the paraphrase directly. Full bibliographic page ranges describe publications; feature-specific page numbers are given only where verified. No page number has been generated from a search snippet’s PDF viewer index.

Research preceded implementation. The initial audit corrected author names, publication types, and access assumptions before the chapter structure was chosen. Sources below are separated by access level. Metadata verification is not equivalent to reading an argument. The unresolved points are retained explicitly so that the team can follow them up through Johns Hopkins library access.

Research findings that determine the design

Construction and physical appearance

Exodus 25:10–22 specifies an acacia chest, gold overlay inside and outside, a gold molding, four rings, gold-covered acacia poles, a pure-gold cover, and two hammered-gold cherubim. It gives the chest’s dimensions as 2½ by 1½ by 1½ cubits. Exodus 37:1–9 narrates the making and names Bezalel. Exodus 40:20–21 describes the testimony, cover, poles, placement, and screening curtain. These passages ground the model, rather than cinema imagery.

The specification is incomplete for a modern modeler: pole length, detailed joinery, surface decoration, exact cherub anatomy, gold thickness, weight, and a universal modern cubit conversion are not supplied. Deuteronomy 10:1–5 describes Moses making a wooden chest and placing tablets inside; its emphasis differs from the elaborate Exodus account. The dossier preserves the comparison without claiming that it alone proves a date or compositional sequence.

Origins and comparative evidence

Scott B. Noegel argues that Egyptian sacred barks offer significant parallels for the Ark’s portability, ritual movement, and divine associations. He proposes Egyptian influence, while acknowledging that no single bark can be identified as the prototype and that the timing and mechanism of transmission are uncertain. See Noegel 2015, especially p. 223 and p. 236. This is a comparative historical hypothesis, not proof that an Egyptian object was converted into the biblical Ark. The game therefore does not display a supposed original Egyptian Ark or an authenticated chain of transmission.

Yigal Levin’s “The Precursors of the Ark” (2022), especially the discussion of 1 Samuel 14:18 and the Septuagint reading “ephod,” offers an argument for multiple local shrine traditions behind later presentations of a single Ark. His interpretation and the textual variant complicate the assumption that every occurrence of an ark in the biblical literature must name one continuous artifact. We do not turn that proposal into a collectible inventory of historically verified arks.

Movements, narrative agency, and presence

Numbers 4 makes access and movement matters of differentiated ritual responsibility. Numbers 10:33–36 associates the traveling Ark with seeking rest and invokes the scattering of enemies. Joshua places it before the people at the Jordan and within the procession at Jericho. In Samuel the Ark can be captured, brings danger among the Philistines, returns without an Israelite military rescue, and becomes dangerous to an Israelite handler as well. The presence it signifies is not simply a controllable instrument of royal or military power.

Maria J. Metzler’s 2016 Harvard dissertation treats the literary complexity of divine agency and violence in Ark narratives, including the experiences of Phinehas’s wife, the returning cows, and Michal. The repository abstract was consulted, not the entire dissertation. Its attention to emotional complexity supports our decision to avoid making affliction, Uzzah’s death, or conquest into entertaining combat rewards. No unverified dissertation page is cited.

History, archaeology, and literary development

Israel Finkelstein and Thomas Römer, “The Historical and Archaeological Background behind the Old Israelite Ark Narrative” (2020), relate 1 Samuel 4:1–7:1 to an eighth-century northern political setting. Their published summary on p. 185 links the transfer from Shiloh to Kiriath-jearim with the aspirations of Jeroboam II’s Israel. This is their historical reconstruction, not an excavated record of the Ark’s actual travels. The game’s routes represent textual sequence; they do not convert site archaeology into proof of the entire narrative.

Römer’s 2023 article “The Mysteries of the Ark of the Covenant” reviews unresolved questions about the Ark’s contents, history, and fate. The university repository record, abstract, and indexed excerpts were accessible; a reliable full-text retrieval was not completed. An indexed discussion proposes a later transfer to Jerusalem in connection with Josiah and reads 2 Chronicles 35:3 in that context. This remains a named hypothesis; the game follows the biblical Solomon installation and labels that choice as a narrative frame. We do not claim that Solomon’s transfer is independently confirmed or that Römer’s proposal represents universal agreement.

Feature 1 — Wood and gold: the camp workshop

Interaction. A controllable character explores a small camp, retrieves two acacia bundles and two gold offerings, and returns them to Bezalel’s bench. A three-strike goldworking activity asks the player to act while a ring glows gold. An early strike loses nothing. When the work is finished, a miniature Ark appears and the northern path opens. Collecting, proximity, navigation, and interaction replace the earlier multiple-choice workshop.

Significance. Offerings and skilled craftsmanship establish the Ark within the sanctuary community. Wood, gold overlay, cover figures, and permanent carrying poles belong to the narrative description rather than the conventions of a treasure chest. The Bezalel dialogue is original screenplay paraphrasing the passages, not an alleged ancient quotation.

Evidence. Exodus 25:1–22; 35:20–29; 37:1–9; 40:20–21. Exodus 25, Exodus 35, Exodus 37. Compare the simpler wooden chest in Deuteronomy 10:1–5. Noegel 2015, pp. 223–224, supplies comparative discussion of the described object; Exodus directly grounds the material choices.

Uncertainties and liberties. Four bundles are a game inventory, not biblical quantities. The timed strikes, bench, collection positions, and exact assembly procedure are inventions. The game does not calculate weight, gold thickness, or an exact modern cubit. The small pixel sprite abbreviates the Ark: it is neither a blueprint nor a proportional 3D reconstruction. Its feather and body shapes do not establish cherub anatomy. The simpler Deuteronomy account remains discussed in this dossier; the live scene follows Exodus without claiming to resolve compositional history.

Feature 2 — Into the wilderness: coverings and carrying

Interaction. Players find the curtain, protective hide, and blue cloth in the camp, returning each to the Ark in sequence. They then take the prepared Ark and walk toward the exit. Additional bearer sprites accompany the player. The game automatically directs the next covering so a player cannot accidentally turn a reading exercise into a different ritual prescription.

Significance. Movement is possible only after the narrated preparation. The short Aaron dialogue distinguishes priestly preparation from Kohathite carrying and warns against touching the sacred objects. The visible group stands for differentiated ritual responsibility, not a single adventurer casually handling the uncovered sacred chest.

Evidence. Numbers 4:5–6, 15, 20; Numbers 10:33–36; Exodus 25:15. Noegel’s comparative discussion of portable ritual objects and Metzler’s abstract on Ark agency provide wider scholarly context; neither authenticates the game’s personnel or route.

Uncertainties and liberties. The avatar is a narrative stand-in for multiple participants, not an independently attested keeper. Actual priestly clothing, number and arrangement of bearers, folds, and load distribution are not reconstructed. The animal identification behind tahash is uncertain; the game uses “hide.” Blue/violet translations vary. Exodus’s poles remaining in place and Numbers’ language of putting them in place are a textual tension retained here rather than solved by the sprite. The slow carrying speed is a gameplay choice, not a measured ancient pace. Tent layout and landscape are imagined.

Feature 3 — The river and the walls: movement in Joshua

Interaction. The player takes the Ark to the Jordan’s edge. At the narrative trigger, a dry crossing becomes traversable; other river areas remain blocked. The bearers stand in the crossing as twelve representative community sprites walk past. After the crossing, players retrieve the Ark and continue to Jericho. Four spatial waypoints guide eight playable circuits. “Walk circuit” follows one circuit automatically; keyboard movement, another destination, or Stop cancels assistance. The same eight circuits and story checkpoints remain. One representative circuit compresses days 1–6, followed by a passing-days montage; seven circuits represent day 7. Joshua 6 describes thirteen circuits; eight is this game’s labeled compression, not a claim about the text. Seven horn bearers, guards, followers, wall-top onlookers, banners, and camp figures enliven the procession. The 3D Ark appears after every playable circuit; Continue returns to the journey. The actual number of depicted pedestrians is a visual shorthand, not the number Joshua gives for the crossing population.

Significance. The Ark leads and remains with the crossing, rather than functioning as a pickup that confers unrestricted water-walking. Repeated movement makes the Jericho schedule a performed narrative pattern. The closing dialogue acknowledges the city’s destruction and Rahab’s preservation; there is no combat, casualty reward, or playable harm.

Evidence. Joshua 3:3–17; Joshua 4:1–9, 18; Joshua 6:1–25. Metzler 2016, repository abstract, frames the broader problem of Ark agency in Joshua and Samuel. It supplies literary context, not archaeological corroboration of these events.

Uncertainties and liberties. The straight river band, crossing width, eight-second crowd passage, city shape, circuit route, and walking speed are designed for play. The first six days share one representative playable circuit. Crowd totals, clothing, banners, camp placement, marching formation, and animations are imagined. No intelligible crowd shouts are added before the final circuit. The scene narrates the text’s claim about stopped water; it offers no physical explanation and no claim of verified miraculous hydrology. The wall is not a reconstruction of an excavated Jericho phase. There is no archaeological claim that a particular discovered destruction establishes Joshua’s story. The doorway-like passage symbolizing onward travel is game navigation, not a surveyed route. No triumphal slaughter sequence is used.

Feature 4 — In foreign hands: Dagon and the returning cart

Interaction. A witness walks through a schematic Ashdod shrine. Interacting beside Dagon advances the first and second mornings. The figure changes from upright to fallen, then to fallen with separated head and hands. Preparing the cart begins a road sequence in which the player guides a cow-drawn cart past Gath, Ekron, Beth-shemesh, and finally Kiriath-jearim. Small chevrons identify the next narrative stop; collisions require finding a route around obstacles.

Significance. Capture does not imply the deity’s defeat within the narrator’s presentation. The story portrays Dagon’s humiliation and the Ark’s return without an Israelite rescue. The opening dialogue identifies Israel’s defeat. Affliction is not a player ability. The cow-cart return can be compared with the wilderness carrying instructions and David’s later procession.

Evidence. 1 Samuel 4:3–11; 1 Samuel 5; 1 Samuel 6; 1 Samuel 7:1. Finkelstein and Römer 2020, p. 185, supports the explicitly attributed northern political interpretation discussed in the research findings. Enemali 2021 has this passage range as its subject, but its unread conclusion is not cited to authorize the gameplay interpretation.

Uncertainties and liberties. The idol is a generic stone-colored humanoid sign; no recovered likeness of Dagon or excavated shrine plan is asserted. Repeated examination after the second morning does not change the story. The road compresses cities into one landscape. The player’s steering and the cart’s attachment to the moving avatar are control conventions; the biblical narrative attributes direction to the cows. Gold offerings are mentioned in dialogue but not modeled as an exact complete inventory. Illness is not diagnosed as any modern disease. The rocks, town buildings, and their positions do not establish the events, dates, or a verified route.

Feature 5 — The road to Jerusalem: interruption and procession

Interaction. Players walk beside the Ark’s cart until a story trigger interrupts the journey. Brief narration describes the stumble, Uzzah’s death, and David’s anger and fear. Players then find Obed-edom’s house. A transition marks three months passing. In the next scene, bearers carry the Ark along a marked procession route to the city gate, where David’s small sprite moves in celebration.

Significance. The arrival in Jerusalem follows an interruption, delay, blessing, and renewed celebration. This prevents the game from presenting a simple uninterrupted victory march. The final carrying imagery can be discussed with Chronicles’ explicit concern for ritual procedure and Levitical responsibility. The live game no longer includes the earlier comparison quiz; the difference between accounts remains an interpretive subject in this dossier.

Evidence. 2 Samuel 6:1–23; 1 Chronicles 13; 1 Chronicles 15:2, 12–15. Compare Numbers 4:15 and 7:9. Metzler 2016, repository abstract, gives context for reading emotional complexity and Michal alongside the other figures, rather than reducing the episode to a transport instruction.

Uncertainties and liberties. No death animation, health loss, or reflex challenge makes Uzzah’s death an entertainment reward. The accident is a narrative trigger, not something a player can prevent or a claim about an electrical property of the Ark. Samuel narrates the anger, fear, three-month stay, and celebration; Chronicles explicitly explains the earlier failure through procedure. That distinction is not erased by depicting bearers in the later scene. Costume, rhythm, dance movements, music, gate layout, and Obed-edom’s house are illustrative. Transitioning from David’s tent to Solomon’s Temple compresses the intervening royal narrative; it does not depict the Temple as already built in David’s procession.

Feature 6 — Beneath the wings: the Temple and ending

Interaction. Players take up the Ark at the entrance and navigate through a schematic succession of rooms, passing openings between dividers and avoiding columns. At the inner platform they set the Ark beneath the large winged figures. The final dialogue describes the tablets, the priests withdrawing, the cloud filling the house, and Solomon’s question about containment. The scene then gives way to the ending and an optional research link.

Significance. The Ark’s installation connects portable sacred presence with the royal sanctuary. Large chamber figures remain visibly distinct from the small cover figures. The ending resists a reduction of divine presence to a deity confined in a box. Its short quotation comes from the World English Bible, 1 Kings 8:27.

Evidence. 1 Kings 6:19–28; 1 Kings 8:1–13, 27; 2 Chronicles 5:2–14. Smith 2020, repository abstract, concerns stone-working and related practical questions based on the Temple description. It does not verify this scene’s architecture. Römer’s discussion opens the separate historical question of transfer chronology; the live story follows Kings’ Solomon frame.

Uncertainties and liberties. The pixel plan is intentionally schematic, not a twenty-cubit architectural scale drawing or a complete Temple model. The dividers and columns serve navigation; their exact arrangement is not claimed as prescribed in Kings. The larger figures signal the olivewood, gold-covered Temple cherubim, but colors and abbreviated shapes cannot establish material composition. Their textual height is ten cubits; the inner sanctuary is described as a twenty-cubit cube. Those dimensions are discussed here rather than silently inferred from the map. Cloud bands are visual narration, not evidence of a recoverable physical phenomenon.

Contents and endpoint. Kings 8:9 specifies only the tablets. Hebrews 9:4 also mentions the manna jar and Aaron’s rod, while Exodus 16:33–34 and Numbers 17 describe placement before the testimony. These textual traditions are not silently merged. The game stops at the installation, without inventing a historical solution to later disappearance or Ethiopian Ark claims.

Feature 7 — Pixel world, avatar, and spatial interaction

Interaction. The entire main screen is the game world. WASD or arrow keys move; E, Space, Enter, or a contextual button interacts. Clicking or tapping an object finds a walkable path to it and interacts on arrival. Touch devices also receive a directional pad. Speed changes while carrying. Narrative dialogue temporarily pauses movement, while a native pause menu gives access to controls, research, scene selection, and restart.

Significance and evidence. Spatial interaction gives the narrative’s material actions—bringing, covering, carrying, standing, circling, and placing—a distinct playable form. The primary bases are the same feature-specific passages cited above. No academic publication is claimed to prescribe the game design or exact controls. Noegel’s comparison and Levin’s discussion remain named scholarly perspectives on the underlying traditions, rather than proof of the sprite designs.

Liberties. The anonymous avatar changes representational role across episodes and is not an attested recurring person. Named-character speech is either labeled adapted screenplay or a specifically linked preserved biblical clause, as detailed in Feature 9. Pixel people, architecture, vegetation, utensils, clothing, roads, water, colors, and animated movement form an imagined visual language. The playfield shows one short objective, sound/pause icons, a nearby action, and paired story dialogue. Repeated chapter names, inventory tallies, hint paragraphs, source classifications, and instructional overlays are omitted from the visible playfield. Verse links remain as small source icons with descriptive accessible labels. Pause has Resume, 3D Ark, Sound, and Help; More contains chapters, Trials, Sources & credits, and Restart. The inline inspector and narrative atlas have been removed from the playfield; a rotatable 3D reconstruction appears between chapters, after each Jericho circuit, at the ending, and through the pause menu, as documented in Feature 11. There is no recovered-object scan or claim to exact cherub iconography. Original synthesized music, wind, footsteps, drums, horns, crowd rustle, and interaction notes start with Play and can be muted. These are modern sound design, not reconstructed ancient music or voice recordings. Web Audio starts within the Play click, following the browser’s user-gesture requirement (MDN Web Audio best practices). Reduced-motion preferences suppress drifting particles and shorten transitions; player-directed walking remains necessary.

Feature 8 — Minimal story interface, checkpoints, and research access

Interaction. During exploration, text is limited to paired Hebrew/English story dialogue with source labels, the current goal, inventory when relevant, and nearby interaction prompts. The title has Play, Keeper Trials, and Help. There is no expedition sidebar, card grid, marketing copy, quiz, percentage progress bar, or field-note collection screen. A small pause menu exposes the separately downloadable research dossier and direct scene selection for classroom use. Checkpoints save the current chapter and subscene in the browser; continuing starts that scene again rather than restoring a partially completed action.

Significance and evidence. Research remains available for scrutiny without becoming mandatory exposition on the playfield. The bibliography’s access audit distinguishes actual consultation from metadata verification. Scene selection follows the literary itinerary of Exodus, Numbers, Joshua, Samuel, and Kings. It does not turn the order of the final narrative into a proven historical chronology.

Liberties and limits. Checkpoints are browser-local, not shared student records. No scores or leaderboard measure acceptance of a theological or scholarly interpretation. There is no location tracking or external app access. The native pause dialog has keyboard dismissal, and menus and story controls have accessible labels. The visual-spatial game is not a fully nonvisual alternative; assistive-technology playtesting has not been performed. The downloadable dossier provides a text account of the narrative and interpretive choices. Research limitations in the bibliography remain applicable to this rebuilt version; the redesign did not make inaccessible full texts available.

## Feature 9 — Hebrew beside English: sourced dialogue

Interaction. Every story speech appears in one paired-language box: unpointed Hebrew in a right-to-left block, with an English rendering beside it. A small link identifies the passage and whether the line is Biblical text or Adapted dialogue. The same Continue action advances both languages together. Spoken instructions, narration, and quoted literary speech are distinguished in the dialogue records; for example, the final question is assigned to Solomon, while the Ark’s capture is narration.

Research method. Short clauses were checked against the Hebrew text presented by Mechon Mamre. Original English translations were prepared for this game; a copyrighted modern English translation was not copied to create the pairs. The ledger below reproduces all 34 story records with their exact passage links. The interface’s English text translates the visible Hebrew, which sometimes condenses the preceding game version’s longer English screenplay.

What the evidence permits. The Hebrew Bible preserves literary accounts, including attributed speeches. It does not supply recordings of Bronze or Iron Age conversations. A preserved clause can be displayed as biblical wording; it cannot establish that a historical individual spoke those exact words on that occasion. New character speeches are therefore labeled adaptations. Bezalel’s requests for materials and Aaron’s concise game instructions are screenplay based on construction and carrying passages, not newly discovered quotations. In particular, divine instructions to Moses or Joshua are not silently relabeled as a direct quotation from Bezalel or Aaron.

Evidence. Exodus 25, Exodus 37, Numbers 4, Joshua 3, Joshua 4, Joshua 6, 1 Samuel 4, 1 Samuel 5, 1 Samuel 6, 1 Samuel 7, 2 Samuel 6, 1 Kings 8. Examples of directly preserved literary speech include Joshua 3:6 and Solomon’s question in 1 Kings 8:27. Examples of preserved narration include 1 Samuel 4:11 and 1 Kings 8:9–10.

Linguistic limits. Unpointed square Hebrew is a readable modern display of the textual tradition, not a reconstruction of an ancient inscriptional script. The game does not reconstruct pronunciation, dialect, or the spoken register of each period. Adaptations use intelligible Hebrew; they are not certified reconstructions of period speech. Philistine dialogue appears in Hebrew as a translation convention because the biblical account gives it in Hebrew; this does not establish that the depicted Philistines spoke Hebrew. No professional native-speaker or historical-linguist review has been completed. Translation and adaptation labels remain visible, and the full primary texts are linked for independent checking.

Feature 10 — Keeper Trials and the battle at Ebenezer

Interaction. Original turn-based encounters use a three-person company—bearer, scout, and horn caller—in place of collectible creatures. Players choose a role and a tactic, manage focus and resolve, read the opposing company’s announced intent, and either open the road or force a withdrawal. Guarding blocks pressure; scouting restores focus; rallying restores resolve; advancing moves the company toward safety. Three optional trials use different intent sequences. Opponents withdraw when resolve fails; there are no killing animations, creature captures, or powers fired from the Ark.

Narrative integration. A short Ebenezer encounter precedes the captured-Ark episode. Its outcome is the Ark’s capture regardless of tactical performance. The player’s role is to respond to a breaking line, not reverse the text’s outcome. Continuing returns to the narrated shrine scene. A player may skip the tactical screen and still encounter the same narrative result. Optional Keeper Trials are visibly labeled fictional and can be entered from the title or pause menu.

Evidence and the answer to “did this happen?” 1 Samuel 4:1–11 narrates a battle against Philistines, Israel’s defeat, and the Ark’s capture. It does not narrate the player’s three-person unit or its tactics. 1 Samuel 6:7–12 describes the Philistines sending the Ark away on a new cart drawn by cows. 1 Samuel 7:1 has the people of Kiriath-jearim bring it to Abinadab’s house. This is not a narrated Israelite combat mission to recapture it. The game consequently offers a battle grounded in the narrative without presenting a victorious recapture as biblical history.

Interpretation. The earlier discussion of Finkelstein and Römer 2020, p. 185, remains relevant to the historical-political interpretation of the Ark narrative. It does not authenticate these tactics. Metzler’s abstract supports attention to narrative agency and the difficulty of treating divine violence as a simple player reward; it does not prescribe a combat system. The battle’s fixed capture outcome also makes the theological problem of treating the Ark as a guaranteed military advantage visible through play.

Creative liberties. All named trials, road raiders, shield companies, skill names, numbers, focus costs, guard values, intent cycles, terrain layouts, crew organization, and victories are game inventions. They are not reconstructions of an excavated battle or an attested ancient military manual. The optional rival companies are fictional opponents, not claims about the inherent character of a historical people. Hebrew tactical commands in Trials are original fictional dialogue with English translations. Only the story result “וארון אלהים נלקח” is presented as preserved wording, from 1 Samuel 4:11. No modern group or historical ethnicity is assigned a moral score through the game mechanics.

Feature 11 — The progress gallery and portable 3D Ark

Interaction. The full-screen original 3D Ark appears after chapters, after every playable Jericho circuit, and at the Temple ending. It is also available through the pause menu’s “View the Ark in 3D.” Continue (or Enter while the model is focused) returns to the preserved journey state. Progress galleries are modern inspection breaks, not narrated ancient events. Dragging, touch gestures, or arrow keys orbit it; plus/minus and focused scrolling zoom it. The simplified gallery shows its progress label, Continue, cover lift, and reset; fuller model documentation and the download remain in the source dossier. A cover-lift button separates the cover and its attached figures, exposing a symbolic interior with two uninscribed tablets. Reset restores the view. A downloadable GLB contains the same original geometry for inspection in compatible 3D software. If WebGL is unavailable, the download remains accessible. The gallery’s quiet controls keep this examination separate from movement and combat.

Evidence. Exodus 25:10–22 supplies the chest’s 2½ × 1½ × 1½ cubit ratios, acacia and gold, molding, four rings, two carrying poles, gold cover, and two cherubim. Exodus 37:1–9 narrates its manufacture. 1 Kings 8:9 supplies the two tablets for the endpoint’s contents. The model does not silently add the manna jar and rod from later textual traditions. The Noegel comparison discussed earlier provides scholarly context for processional furniture, not a recovered model of this object.

Material comparison. The Grand Egyptian Museum’s record for Ritual Carrying Box, GEM 4881, describes an Egyptian chest with four sliding poles, red-wood panels, ebony frames, and ritual associations. Museum catalogue. It independently documents ancient carrying furniture. It is not the biblical Ark, is not identical in its materials or pole arrangement, and does not prove direct derivation. The game’s model follows Exodus’s two-pole description rather than copying the museum object.

Reconstruction limits. Geometry is newly made, not imported from an alleged excavated Ark. The chest’s prescribed proportions are retained, while thicknesses, seams, molding profiles, pole length, exact ring positions, shine, feather segmentation, and composite cherub anatomy are interpretive. The tablets are symbolic and uninscribed: their exact shape and appearance are not recoverable from this specification. The cover-lift animation is a modern examination device, not an attested ritual performance. Renderer lighting is illustrative; metal colors do not establish an actual alloy. The large Temple cherubim remain distinct from the small cover figures. The model is not presented as proof of the object’s historicity, contents at every period, or later fate.

Questions for the final university paper

  • What changes when a ritual object’s story is told from the viewpoint of priests, royal ideology, threatened communities, or affected individuals?
  • How do Exodus’s ornate construction and Deuteronomy’s simpler description shape the reader’s understanding of covenant and presence?
  • When is an analogy between artifacts informative, and what additional evidence would be needed to infer actual cultural transmission?
  • What would count as material evidence for the Ark itself, rather than evidence that a named place existed or hosted ritual activity?
  • How do the differences between Samuel and Chronicles complicate an uninterrupted historical itinerary?
  • What is lost when a game turns divine violence or conquest into a triumph mechanic, and how can a presentation preserve the difficult material responsibly?
